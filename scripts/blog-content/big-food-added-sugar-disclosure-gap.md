Since 2016, the FDA has required packaged foods to declare **added sugars** on the Nutrition Facts label — separately from total sugars, with a percent Daily Value. It is the single most useful number on the panel, because it distinguishes the sugar that was in the food from the sugar somebody put there.

Restaurant and chain menu items are not packaged foods. **They do not have to declare added sugars at all.** Chains over 20 locations must post calories, and provide certain nutrients on request, but the added-sugar line — the one the FDA created specifically because consumers could not otherwise tell — is absent from the counter.

That is not a conspiracy. It is a scope decision in a regulation. But it produces a strange result: the identical food is transparent in a grocery aisle and opaque at a register, and the categories built almost entirely on sugar are disproportionately sold at registers.

## The gap, demonstrated

We have now researched a dozen seasonal foods for this site. The pattern was consistent enough to tabulate.

| Product | Added sugar published? | What we could establish |
|---|---|---|
| Cinnabon Classic Roll | **Yes** — company guide | 880 cal, 61 g sugar, **59 g added** |
| Coffee mate creamer | **Yes** — packaged label | Water, corn syrup solids, soybean oil; no cream |
| Tazo / Oregon Chai concentrate | **Yes** — packaged label | 24 g and 28 g added sugar per serving |
| Happy Apple / Tastee caramel apple | **Yes** — packaged label | Corn syrup is the first caramel ingredient in both |
| Starbucks PSL | **No** | ~32 g added, per CSPI's independent 2025 analysis |
| Starbucks Chai Latte | **No** | 29 g total sugars; added portion never disclosed |
| Dunkin' Apple Cider Donut | **No** | Unresolvable — see below |

Notice the split. Every product sold in a package discloses. Every product sold across a counter does not. The companies behaving "well" and "badly" here are mostly just companies selling through different channels under different rules.

## The cider donut, which nobody can answer

This is the clearest case we found, and it is worth walking through because it is not a scandal — it is an absence.

Dunkin' publishes downloadable nutrition documents but does not display per-item sugar detail for its seasonal Apple Cider Donut. So the figures circulating online come from third-party aggregators, and they contradict each other. One widely cited database lists it at **380 calories, 22 g fat, 41 g carbohydrate**, with no sugar line at all and a page last updated in 2020. Another lists **320 calories, 18 g fat, 37 g carbohydrate and 15 g total sugars**. Neither reports added sugars.

Sixty calories apart, on a single donut, from sources people are treating as authoritative.

For reference, USDA's entry for a plain 60-gram cake doughnut — before any cinnamon-sugar coating — is 260 calories, 15 g fat and 11 g total sugars. A cider donut is coated. So the honest answer to "how much sugar is in a Dunkin' cider donut" is: **more than 11 grams, by an amount nobody has published.** Orchard and farmstand versions, which is what most people picture, have no published data whatsoever.

We are not going to invent a number to fill that gap, and the fact that we cannot is the story.

The disclosure gap cuts both ways. When a company publishes, you can be specific — and specificity is fairer than insinuation.

Cinnabon publishes 59 grams of added sugar in one Classic Roll. That is more than the AHA's entire daily limit for a man, and more than twice the limit for a woman, in a single pastry — and we know it precisely **because Cinnabon publishes it.** The company being most transparent in this set also has the highest number in it. That is worth sitting with before treating disclosure as an indictment.

## The ingredient-order tell

Where labels exist, the ingredient list is often more informative than the nutrition panel, because ingredients are ordered by weight.

- **Coffee mate The Original** lists water, corn syrup solids and soybean oil. There is no cream in the coffee creamer. That is a naming convention, not a violation, but it is the kind of thing a label reveals and a menu board cannot.
- **Happy Apple and Tastee Apple caramel apples** both begin their caramel with corn syrup, followed by sugar or cane sugar and sweetened condensed milk, with hydrogenated soybean oil further down.
- **Oregon Chai Original** lists cane sugar syrup ahead of the tea. The sweetener outweighs the thing the product is named after.
- **Fruit snacks** vary more than any category we looked at. Some are 100% added sugar with juice concentrate for color and flavor; Welch's, to be accurate about it, genuinely does list whole fruit purée first.

None of these are illegal or hidden. They are printed on the package. The gap is not that companies lie on labels — it is that most people never read them, and that the products where reading would matter most are frequently sold without one.

## Why this matters more than any single product

CDC's National Center for Health Statistics reports that **ultra-processed foods supply 53.0% of calories for US adults**. That is the number that gives the labeling question its weight. If half of what a population eats comes from products formulated by companies, the disclosure rules governing those products are not a technicality — they are most of what a person can know about their own diet.

And the health association is not speculative for the sugary-drink slice of it. Malik and colleagues, in *Diabetes Care*, pooled 310,819 participants and 15,043 cases and found people in the highest category of sugar-sweetened beverage intake had a **26% greater risk of developing type 2 diabetes** than those consuming almost none. Later dose-response work puts the increase near 27% per additional daily serving.

To be careful about what that does and does not show: it is observational, it is about habitual daily intake rather than occasional consumption, and confounding in nutrition epidemiology is real and well documented. It is a strong signal about a pattern, not a verdict on a cup.

## What to actually do with this

Three things, in order of how much they change:

- **Read the ingredient list before the panel.** It is ordered by weight, it cannot be rounded the way small nutrient quantities can, and it tells you what the product is rather than what it contains.
- **At a counter, assume the sugar is in the syrup or the sauce.** In every drink we examined, that is where it lived — not in the coffee, the tea or the milk. Reducing pumps is the only counter-side change that moves the number much.
- **When the number is unpublished, treat it as unknown rather than low.** The absence of a figure is not evidence of a small one.

The practical alternative is not asceticism. Nobody craves corn syrup or a shelf-stable concentrate; what people want is warmth, sweetness and a ritual, and all of that survives being made properly. ## Frequently asked questions

### Do restaurants have to list sugar content?

Not added sugar. Chain restaurants with 20 or more locations must post calorie counts on menus and menu boards, and must make additional nutrition information — including total sugars — available on request. But the added-sugars declaration that the FDA requires on packaged Nutrition Facts labels does not extend to restaurant menu items, so the figure distinguishing inherent from added sugar is generally unavailable at a counter.

### Why does added sugar matter more than total sugar?

Because total sugar includes sugar that was already in the food. The lactose in milk and the fructose in whole fruit arrive inside a matrix of protein, fat, water and fiber. Added sugar is what a manufacturer put in. Dietary guidance is written about the added portion — the AHA's limits of 25 g for women and 36 g for men, and the 2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines' recommendation to stay under 10% of daily calories — which is precisely why the FDA broke it out as its own line in 2016.

### Is "ultra-processed" the same as unhealthy?

No, and the term is doing a lot of work it was not designed for. Ultra-processed is a classification about industrial formulation and ingredients not found in a home kitchen, not a direct measure of nutritional quality — some ultra-processed foods are fortified staples. The 53.0% figure from CDC/NCHS is worth knowing as a description of how the food supply is built, not as a claim that half of what Americans eat is poison.

### Which companies are the worst offenders?

That framing does not survive the data. In this set, the company disclosing the highest added-sugar figure is the one being most transparent, and the products with no figure at all are not necessarily worse — we simply cannot say. The pattern that holds is structural: packaged products disclose because they must, counter-sold products do not because they need not. That is a rules question more than a villains question.
