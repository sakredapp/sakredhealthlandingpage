This is the topic in the Big Food series where we know the least, and we are going to say so up front rather than at the bottom.

Here is what is not in dispute: **bisphenol A and phthalates are detectable in the overwhelming majority of the American food supply.** Consumer Reports' 2024 testing found phthalates in 99% of the supermarket and fast foods it sampled and BPA in 79%. Both classes of compound interact with hormone receptors in laboratory systems, which is why they are called endocrine disruptors.

Here is what is genuinely unsettled: **whether the amounts people actually consume cause harm.** And the strongest evidence of how unsettled it is comes from the regulators themselves. In April 2023, the European Food Safety Authority cut its tolerable daily intake for BPA from 4,000 nanograms per kilogram of body weight to **0.2 nanograms — a 20,000-fold reduction** — and concluded that dietary exposure to BPA is a health concern. Germany's Federal Institute for Risk Assessment reviewed the same evidence and set a limit **1,000 times higher** than EFSA's. The European Medicines Agency filed a formal disagreement. The US Food and Drug Administration maintains that BPA is safe at the levels found in food.

Four expert bodies. The same published literature. Conclusions four orders of magnitude apart.

Anyone who tells you this question is settled — in either direction — is telling you something the evidence does not support. What follows is our best attempt to describe the disagreement accurately instead of picking the side that makes a better headline.

## Why they disagree

The dispute is methodological, not a matter of one side ignoring the data.

**EFSA's approach** was to define a protocol in advance and then include *all* relevant studies, both guideline (standardized, industry-run toxicology tests) and non-guideline (academic studies using varied designs). Doing that, EFSA identified an increase in Th17 immune cells at very low BPA exposures and used that endpoint to derive the new limit.

**BfR's objection** is that including non-guideline studies departs from established hazard-characterization practice. Guideline studies follow standardized protocols specifically so results can be compared and replicated; academic studies vary in design, dose selection and quality, and folding them into a formal risk assessment is, in BfR's view, methodologically unsound.

Both positions are defensible. EFSA's is that restricting analysis to industry-standard tests systematically misses low-dose effects those tests were never designed to detect — a serious criticism, since endocrine disruptors are hypothesized to act at low doses in non-linear ways. BfR's is that loosening evidentiary standards produces limits that no one can replicate or enforce.

We are not qualified to adjudicate that, and we are not going to pretend otherwise.

## What is actually in food

Consumer Reports tested 85 foods in 2024 and reported phthalates in all but one, at levels from under 1,000 ng per serving to more than 53,000 ng in a canned pasta product. BPA appeared in 79% of samples.

Two honest observations about that testing.

First, **Consumer Reports did not report that these levels exceed current regulatory limits.** Their argument is different and should be represented accurately: they contend the thresholds themselves are outdated. Their scientist Tunde Akinleye put it as *"Many of these thresholds do not reflect the most current scientific knowledge, and may not protect against all the potential health effects."* That is an advocacy position about the adequacy of standards, not a finding that a legal limit was breached. It may well be correct. It is a different claim.

Second, the contamination **did not track packaging type the way people assume.** Consumer Reports found these chemicals widespread regardless of whether the food came in plastic — which points at the whole production chain, including processing equipment, tubing, gloves and conveyor belts, rather than at the container alone. That undercuts the intuitive fix of simply avoiding plastic packaging.

## What "endocrine disruptor" does and doesn't mean

The term describes a *mechanism*, not a demonstrated *outcome*. A compound earns the label by interacting with hormone receptors in laboratory systems. That is a real property, and it is a legitimate reason for regulatory attention.

It is not the same as a demonstrated effect on human health at real-world doses. The gap between "binds a receptor in a dish" and "causes disease in people eating normal diets" is exactly where this entire controversy lives.

**Reasonably established:**
- BPA and phthalates are present in most of the food supply
- Both interact with hormone receptors in laboratory systems
- Human exposure is essentially universal in industrialized countries
- Regulators disagree profoundly about what exposure level is acceptable

**Not established:**
- That current dietary exposures cause disease in humans
- That any specific health problem in a given person is attributable to them
- That any particular consumer swap meaningfully reduces total exposure
- Which of the four regulatory positions above is correct

Notice how much sits in the second list. That is not us hedging — it is the actual state of the field, and it is why this post makes no recommendations dressed up as science.

## What we are not going to tell you

We are not going to tell you to throw out your plastic containers, buy a particular brand of cookware, or take a supplement to "detox" these compounds. There is no evidence that would justify any of it, and the wellness industry's confidence on this topic vastly exceeds the evidence base.

What can be said without overstepping is modest and mostly free: this is one more reason that food prepared from whole ingredients passes through fewer industrial systems than food assembled in a factory — which is the same conclusion the rest of this section reaches by better-evidenced routes, like [the calorie effect of ultra-processed food](/blog/do-ultra-processed-foods-make-you-eat-more) and [what sugary drinks do to the liver](/blog/do-sugary-drinks-cause-insulin-resistance). If the packaging chemistry turns out to matter, that habit already accounts for it. If it turns out not to, nothing was lost.

That is a genuinely weaker claim than "Big Food is poisoning your hormones." It is the one the evidence supports.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is BPA dangerous in food?

Regulators disagree sharply. EFSA concluded in 2023 that dietary BPA exposure is a health concern and set a tolerable daily intake 20,000 times lower than its previous figure. Germany's BfR set a limit 1,000 times higher than EFSA's, the European Medicines Agency formally objected, and the FDA maintains BPA is safe at levels found in food.

### Are phthalates in food regulated in the US?

Partly. In May 2022 the FDA revoked authorization for 25 plasticizer substances, including 23 phthalates, because industry had stopped using them. Nine phthalates remain authorized for food-contact use, and in 2024 the FDA reaffirmed it would not prohibit them.

### Does avoiding plastic packaging reduce exposure?

Less than people expect. Consumer Reports found these chemicals widespread across foods regardless of packaging type, which suggests contamination enters through processing equipment as well as containers.

### Do these chemicals affect testosterone or fertility?

That is the hypothesis behind the concern, based on receptor-level activity in laboratory systems. It has not been established that current dietary exposures produce those effects in humans, and claims that a specific person's hormone levels are explained by food packaging go well beyond the evidence.

### Should I be worried?

We don't think this evidence supports either alarm or dismissal. It supports paying attention while the regulators work it out — and noting that the case for cooking from whole ingredients already rests on better-established ground.
