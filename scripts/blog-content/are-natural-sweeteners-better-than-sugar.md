Mostly no — but not uniformly, and the differences that are real are not the ones being advertised.

Here is the short version before the detail. **The only sweetener on the shelf with a genuinely different metabolic profile is the one that isn't really a sweetener: whole dates and date sugar, which are dried fruit with the fiber still in them.** Maple syrup and honey are real foods carrying trace minerals, but the sugar in them is free sugar and behaves like it. **Coconut sugar's famous low glycemic index rests on a single industry-funded study of 10 people that has never been independently replicated.** And **agave — marketed hardest of all on its low GI — is roughly 85% fructose**, which is more than high-fructose corn syrup, and fructose is precisely the sugar a controlled trial showed doubles the liver's fat production.

That last one is the inversion worth remembering: the sweetener with the lowest glycemic index in this group is arguably the one with the weakest case, because glycemic index only measures blood glucose, and fructose barely raises blood glucose *because the liver takes it instead*.

## Why glycemic index is the wrong yardstick on its own

Glycemic index measures one thing: how much a food raises **blood glucose** in the two hours after eating it. That is genuinely useful information, and it is also incomplete in a way the sweetener market has learned to exploit.

Fructose does not raise blood glucose much. It therefore scores a low glycemic index. But the reason it scores low is not that the body handles it gently — it is that **fructose is routed largely to the liver instead of circulating as blood sugar.** A low GI here reflects a different metabolic destination, not a gentler one.

This is where the controlled evidence matters. In a double-blind trial, 94 adults drank 80 grams of sugar daily for seven weeks — fructose, sucrose, glucose, or none. Fructose and sucrose **roughly doubled** the liver's production of new fat. The same dose of glucose did nothing to it. We covered that trial in full in [what sugary drinks actually do](/blog/do-sugary-drinks-cause-insulin-resistance), including the part most coverage omits: the same trial found **no** evidence of adipose insulin resistance over those seven weeks.

So a sweetener marketed as "low glycemic" because it is mostly fructose is being sold on a technicality. Agave is the clearest case.

## What actually makes dates different

Dates are the exception, and it is worth being precise about why rather than treating them as a health food.

They are **more than 70% sugar.** Nobody should pretend otherwise, and any recipe telling you a date-sweetened dessert is sugar-free is selling something.

And yet: across five varieties tested in both healthy and type 2 diabetic subjects, dates measured a **glycemic index of roughly 44 to 55** — low-to-medium — with no significant post-meal glucose excursions in the diabetic group. The mechanism isn't mysterious. The fiber the fruit came with is still present, and it slows the rate at which those sugars arrive.

The critical distinction, which almost nothing on a supermarket shelf makes clear:

- **Date sugar** is dehydrated whole dates, milled. The fiber is still there. It behaves like the fruit.
- **Date syrup** has had the fiber strained out. It behaves like a syrup.

Same fruit, different product, genuinely different result. If a label says syrup, the advantage is gone.

## What about blood pressure?

Worth separating carefully, because the evidence here is much weaker than the glucose evidence and gets quoted as though it were stronger.

The well-supported chain is: **sugar-sweetened beverages → weight gain and metabolic changes → cardiovascular risk factors including blood pressure.** That is largely observational, and it concerns total sugar intake and the beverages carrying it — not the choice between maple and cane sugar.

What is **not** established is that swapping one caloric sweetener for another meaningfully changes blood pressure. There is no good trial evidence that a person eating the same quantity of sugar from coconut rather than cane will see a different blood pressure reading. Anyone claiming a specific sweetener lowers blood pressure is well past the data.

The honest summary: **quantity is doing nearly all the work here; sweetener choice is a second-order effect, and only dates change the picture much at all.**

## What we do in our recipes, and why

We sweeten with whole dates where the texture allows it, and with small measured amounts of maple or coconut sugar where it does not. Those are real decisions with real reasoning behind them, and they are also modest.

Two things we will not tell you:

**That our sugar is real and theirs is artificial.** It isn't. Starbucks' Classic Syrup is sugar and water; Cinnabon and Swiss Miss use real cane sugar. The difference between a commercial product and a home version is the *quantity* and *what the sugar arrives inside* — not real versus fake. Claiming otherwise is the easiest possible thing for a critic to disprove, and we would rather make the argument that survives.

**That natural sweeteners are metabolically free.** FDA counts dates, maple, honey, coconut sugar and agave as added sugars, and so do we — which is why every recipe on this site prints its added-sugar figure *and* its total-sugar figure, including the ones where the total is not flattering.

The claim we will make: **less sugar, and where the recipe allows, sugar that still has its fiber.** That one is true.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is coconut sugar healthier than regular sugar?

Barely, if at all. It is roughly 70–80% sucrose, chemically close to table sugar. Its widely marketed glycemic index of 35 comes from a single 2003 study of 10 subjects funded by the Philippine coconut industry, never independently replicated. Later testing at the University of Sydney measured 54, against 65 for table sugar.

### Is agave nectar a good sugar substitute?

It has the lowest glycemic index of the common natural sweeteners and arguably the weakest case for using it. Agave is roughly 85% fructose — more than high-fructose corn syrup — and its low GI reflects the fact that fructose goes to the liver rather than the bloodstream, not that the body handles it gently.

### Is date sugar actually different from sugar?

Yes, and it is the one clear exception. Date sugar is dried whole dates ground up, so the fruit's fiber is still present. Dates are over 70% sugar yet measure a glycemic index of about 44–55. Date *syrup* has the fiber strained out and loses that advantage.

### Does honey raise blood sugar less than sugar?

Not meaningfully. Honey is roughly half glucose and half fructose, similar to table sugar's composition, and FDA counts it as an added sugar.

### Do natural sweeteners affect blood pressure differently?

There is no good trial evidence that swapping one caloric sweetener for another changes blood pressure. The established links run from total sugar intake and sugary drinks to weight and cardiometabolic risk — quantity, not the type on the label.
