# Sakred Health editorial standard

## What we are

Holistic health, alternative medicine, Eastern medicine, wellness. **We are not a medical
publication and we do not give medical advice.** We do not defer to institutions because
they are institutions.

## The differentiator

Unopinionated study summaries already exist everywhere. Nobody needs another one, and
nobody will come to us for one. What does not exist is the layer on top: someone who reads
the study, digests it, and tells you **what narrative it is being used to push and what it
made you stop looking at.**

That is the product. Every substantive post should have it.

## The seven-step pattern

Apply this anywhere a study is cited to settle a question.

1. **Name the topic and why people are asking.** No throat-clearing.
2. **Report the finding honestly and in full.** Do not strip the study out, do not soften
   the number, do not bury it below our objection. If it is inconvenient, say it plainly
   first. Credibility is the whole asset.
3. **Translate the endpoint into plain language.** What did it *actually* measure? Usually:
   *"does changing one input, over a few weeks, in a controlled population, move one blood
   marker?"* Write that sentence out. It is almost always narrower than the headline.
4. **Name the narrative.** What does the finding get reported as? Who benefits from that
   reading? A null result on the wrong endpoint gets sold as a clean bill of health.
5. **Name what it never measured** — the outcomes a holistic reader actually cares about:
   fascia, mobility, digestion, sleep, energy, how you feel at 65, what the food does to
   the body over thirty years, what the processing did to the ingredient.
   **"The study did not find X. It never looked."**
6. **State our position and what it rests on.** Whole, unprocessed, unrefined, natural of
   the earth. Say explicitly when our position does not depend on the biomarker — because
   it usually doesn't, and that is a strength, not a dodge.
7. **Concede in both directions.** Do not claim what we cannot show. "Nobody has tested
   what people actually buy" is a damning sentence on its own and does not need inflating
   into "what people buy is harmful."

## Endpoint mismatch — the most common failure

The single most frequent trick is measuring the wrong outcome and reporting the null as a
verdict.

> Standing desks did not improve cardiovascular outcomes. Nobody stands at a desk to lower
> their blood pressure. Standing is not cardio. The reason to stand is what eight hours of
> hip flexion does over thirty years — and that was never measured.

Whenever a study reports a null, ask: **was that even the reason anyone does this?**

## Processing is a variable studies almost never control

Trials match calories, macros, and fatty-acid profiles. They almost never isolate what the
refining chain did. Make the contrast concrete and physical:

- Olive oil: crush the fruit, press it.
- Butter: agitate cream until the fat clusters. You can do it in a jar.
- Canola/soy/corn: crack, flake, heat, press, hexane wash, degum, neutralize, bleach,
  deodorize by steam-stripping at ~200°C.

Whole food vs. isolate is the same move: the collagen trials tested a milled peptide
extract, not broth. Null result on the isolate is not a verdict on the food.

## Product heterogeneity

A trial specifies its material — defined batch, stored to protocol, fresh. The supermarket
version has none of that: oxidation state, reuse, blends, shelf time, none of it on the
label. **The trials tested something the shopper cannot buy, and the shopper buys something
the trials never tested.**

## Sourcing rules

- **No WHO.** Not as a citation, not laundered through a different host that publishes the
  same document. If a concept originates with WHO, say so honestly rather than disguising
  the provenance.
- EFSA, CDC/NCHS, AHA, FDA, USDA are acceptable as *data* sources — labels, surveillance
  numbers, regulatory facts. They are not authorities whose guidance we adopt.
- Prefer primary literature, and link it.
- Industry-funded trials get flagged as industry-funded.

## Sugar

Our sweeteners are **real**: dates, coconut sugar, maple, raw honey. Never imply
equivalence with artificial sweeteners. Never call a date-sweetened recipe sugar-free.
Always name which sweetener, and link to the sweetener comparison post.

## Fats and oils

**Extra-virgin olive oil and unrefined (virgin) coconut oil.** Organic where possible.
Never write "neutral oil" — at a supermarket that means canola. Never specify a refined
oil. Butter is a natural dairy product and is fine.

## Tone

Direct, specific, unhedged where we have grounds and explicitly uncertain where we don't.
Call out tunnel vision by name. Never moralize, never scold the reader, never pretend a
single biomarker settles whether something is good for a human being.
